# Presenting like a professional

Write for both the recipient who can resolve the issue and also to build a clear paper trail that can be presented to a party such as a regulatory agency such that they can easily bring an enforcement action later if there is some law/regulation that is violated.

## Approach

Inspired by Patrick McKenzie's [Identity Theft, Credit Reports, and You](https://www.kalzumeus.com/2017/09/09/identity-theft-credit-reports/) (2017), particularly its discussion of professional presentation and written records:

- Be calm, factual, and definite about the requested resolution. Avoid insults, emotional argument, and unnecessary apologies.
- Identify the specific action, request written confirmation, and give a reasonable response date.
- If the recipient declines, request their factual or contractual explanation in writing.
- Follow up by referencing the earlier request and what remains unanswered. Preserve correspondence and delivery evidence.
- Escalate to an appropriate decision-maker when ordinary support cannot resolve the matter. Make only truthful statements about actions already taken or genuinely intended.

The article concerns US credit-reporting disputes. Use its communication approach, not its legal conclusions, deadlines, or predictions of success, for other subjects. Verify current law independently before citing it.

## Build the message from the record

Read the relevant thread and supporting notices before drafting. Make a compact chronology of the transaction, problem, requested remedy, responses, and outstanding action. Distinguish firsthand observations, the other party's statements, and inferences. Preserve facts that weaken the user's position; address them directly rather than drafting around them.

Identify whether the basis is a contractual promise, a verified legal requirement, or a request for discretionary accommodation. A potential statutory deficiency is a concern to explain, not automatically an established violation or entitlement to a refund. Check jurisdiction, effective dates, coverage, and remedy before asserting legal rights. Do not carry claims from the examples into a new dispute.

Usually use this shape, adapted to the situation:

1. State the unresolved issue and requested remedy.
2. Give only the facts needed to assess it: dates, amount, prior requests, and relevant promise or requirement.
3. Explain why the last response did not resolve the issue, if applicable.
4. Request concrete next actions and written confirmation. If declined, ask for the specific reason and relevant supporting records.
5. Set a proportionate response date when useful. Distinguish a requested deadline from a legal deadline. Do not imply silence means agreement.

Use short paragraphs and ordinary language. “Please refund…” or “Please confirm…” is usually firm enough. Use “require” when the underlying obligation supports it. Avoid boilerplate threats, inflated legal language, and claims to professional or legal status the user does not have. Mention a possible escalation only when useful and accurate; do not invent an intention to sue or file a complaint.

## Make a usable paper trail

Reply in the existing thread when it preserves context. Include the relevant case or transaction reference, but exclude unnecessary personal information. Cite the earlier message's date and the precise outstanding request. Refer to evidence already supplied; attach it again only when helpful and authorized.

For calls, record the date, participants, substantive statements, commitments, and next steps. A follow-up email can confirm the user's understanding and invite correction; do not describe an unconfirmed understanding as an agreement.

When asked to maintain a case record, retain original messages and evidence separately from a concise chronology. Record sending and delivery evidence, deadlines, responses, and verified completion. A draft is not sent correspondence, a promised refund is not a received refund, and a company's claim of payment is not independent payment verification. Delivery tracking supports delivery, not necessarily the exact contents of an envelope.

Drafting does not authorize sending, filing complaints, contacting additional parties, or creating recurring reminders. Carry out those steps only within the user's instructions. Do not automatically impose a new approval step on actions already authorized.
